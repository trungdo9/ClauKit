# ORB — Owned, Rented, Borrowed Channels

Structure launch marketing across three channel types. Everything ultimately leads back to owned channels.

## Owned

You own the channel (though never the audience). Direct access, no algorithm, no platform rules.

**Examples:** email list, blog, podcast, branded community (Slack/Discord), website and the product itself.

**Why they matter:** they get more effective over time; no algorithm change or pay-to-play tax; direct relationship; content compounds.

**Start with 1–2, chosen by audience:**
- Industry lacks quality content → start a blog.
- People want direct updates → focus on email.
- Engagement is the differentiator → build a community.

**Case — Superhuman:** built demand through an invite-only waitlist plus one-on-one onboarding — every new user got a 30-minute live demo. Exclusivity, FOMO, and word of mouth, all through owned relationships. Years later the original onboarding materials still drive engagement.

## Rented

Platforms that give visibility you don't control. Algorithms shift, rules change, pay-to-play creeps up.

**Examples:** social platforms (X/Twitter, LinkedIn, Instagram), YouTube, Reddit, app stores and marketplaces.

**How to use correctly:**
- Pick 1–2 platforms where the audience is already active.
- Use them to drive traffic into owned channels.
- Never let them be the whole strategy.

**Platform tactics:**
- X/Twitter — threads that spark conversation, linking to the newsletter.
- LinkedIn — high-value posts leading to gated content or an email signup.
- Marketplaces (Shopify, Slack, etc.) — optimize the listing, drive to the site for the rest.

**Case — Notion:** hacked virality on Twitter, YouTube, and Reddit where productivity enthusiasts lived, encouraging the community to share templates and workflows — but funnelled every viral post into signups and then targeted email onboarding.

Rented channels give speed, not stability. Capture the momentum by bringing users into the owned ecosystem.

## Borrowed

Someone else's audience — the shortcut past the hardest part, getting noticed.

**Examples:** guest content (blog posts, podcast interviews, newsletter features), collaborations (webinars, co-marketing, social takeovers), speaking (conferences, panels, virtual summits), influencer partnerships.

**Be proactive, not passive:**
1. List the industry leaders your audience already follows.
2. Pitch win-win collaborations, not favors.
3. Use audience-overlap research (e.g. SparkToro, Listen Notes) to find where they actually are.
4. Set up affiliate/referral incentives so the promotion continues after the launch week.

**Case — TRMNL:** sent a free e-ink display to YouTuber Snazzy Labs — no paid sponsorship, just hoping he'd like it. The resulting in-depth review passed 500K views and drove $500K+ in sales. They also ran an affiliate program for ongoing promotion.

Borrowed channels give instant credibility, but only work if borrowed attention is converted into owned relationships.

## Applying ORB to a launch plan

For each launch, fill the three rows with *specific* assets and numbers, not categories:

| Type | Asset | Current reach | Launch role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owned | e.g. newsletter | e.g. 500 subs | announcement send, day-of + day-3 |
| Rented | e.g. X account | e.g. 2k followers | launch thread, demo clip |
| Borrowed | e.g. 3 podcasts, 1 community | — | pre-launch interviews, day-of post |

Thin owned rows mean the pre-launch phases must spend their time building the list, not polishing assets.
